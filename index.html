I

about

applying to study data science
❦Daniel FuSelected Works❦

On paper my activities look scattered. Most of them turned out to be one job on different data: finding what a model missed.

In finance it was the profit or loss left after a hedge that, on paper, cancels the risk. In medicine it is the patient a blood test misses. In statistics it is the outliers someone trimmed and then forgot they had trimmed. In robotics the numbers talk back fastest, and a robot that drifts left tells you exactly what your code assumed.

The habit started at a drawing table. When you draw from memory, you draw your idea of an eye. My drawings only got better when I sat in front of a real person and drew the shadow under the lower lid, the one my idea of an eye had no room for.

Outside research I coach robotics, teach skiing at Blue Mountain, box heavyweight, lift, and read two or three books a week, mostly Baldwin, Camus and Woolf.

R1accepted11th Derivative Markets Conference, Auckland · 2026

What Survives a Delta Hedge? Curvature, Jump Exposure, and the Maturity Clock in SPX Options

Craig Vishwesh, Tanya Otsetarova (Citadel Securities), Daniel Fu · third author

Large hedging errors near expiry turn out to be mainly a curvature effect, not jumps. We separate the discrete-hedging floor from whatever jumps add on top of it, using intraday data on S&P 500 options that expire the same day. The answer was a null. Instead of digging until something showed up, we wrote down what our test could not detect. The paper also earned an interview offer from MarketWatch.

my partI checked the dataset against its own documentation and two claims failed: the file was less than half its described size, and one daily record belonged to the next day's contract. I helped test the result against 340 simulated markets with no jumps in them, and built the figures and replication code.

R3presentedMayo Clinic 18th Annual Stroke & Cerebrovascular Disease Review · oct 2026

Beyond Lesion Burden: Circulating PINK1/Parkin Distinguishes Vascular Parkinsonism and Predicts Incident Parkinsonism in Cerebral Small-Vessel Disease

Craig V. Vishwesh, Anbu Benzigar, Charlotte DeLong, Daniel Fu, Yinuo Zhang, Kyle Prekeris, Ryan Huang · fourth author and poster presenter · Benzigar, DeLong and Prekeris are from Johnson & Johnson

Why do some patients with the same amount of small-vessel damage in the brain develop vascular parkinsonism while others don't? We compared blood levels of two mitophagy proteins, PINK1 and Parkin, across 118 patients with vascular parkinsonism, 118 controls matched on lesion burden, and 92 with Parkinson's disease. Adding both markers to clinical and imaging data raised diagnostic AUC from 0.75 to 0.84, and in a separate cohort of 452 patients followed for 30 months, baseline PINK1 predicted who went on to develop parkinsonism (hazard ratio 1.74 per standard deviation). In medicine, the leftover is a patient the test misses.

R4preprintZenodo · 2026

Improved Lower Bounds for Sárközy's Theorem in Function Fields from Independent Sets in Products of Generalized Paley Graphs

Daniel Fu (first author), Ryan David (Monroe Township High School)

Sárközy's theorem says that any large enough set of whole numbers contains two that differ by a perfect square, cube or higher power. The open question is how large a set can be while avoiding that. We work in the polynomial version of the problem over a finite field, where Naslund built large avoiding sets out of independent sets in Paley graphs. How big his sets get depends on one number, the independence number rk,2(Fq) of a product graph, which had only been worked out in a few special cases.

We computed it exactly for cubes over F13: it is 21. That gives cube-difference-free sets of size about 130.8645n, the largest exponent this method has produced for cubes, up from Naslund's 5/6 at q = 13 and 0.8639 at q = 7. We also prove an infinite family where the answer can be worked out by hand, and give verified sets that raise the exponent for fourth powers over F17 (0.875 → 0.9138) and seventh powers over F29 (0.9286 → 0.9618).

how we knowTwo independent exhaustive computations agree: a branch-and-bound search (1.9 million nodes, about two seconds) and a SAT-solver proof that no 22-element set exists. Before trusting either, we had the code recompute five values already in the literature. Every certificate is checked by a standalone script included in the paper, and a Lovász theta bound shows where the method itself runs out: 0.8805 for q = 13.

two papers you can turn the knobs on · simulated data

hedge more often, see what's left

from R1 · what survives a delta hedge

Sell an at-the-money S&P option at the open that expires at the close, and delta-hedge it through the day. On paper the hedge cancels the risk, so what's left at the close should be zero. It never is. Hedging at set times leaves a floor, and jumps add more on top.

spread of what's left–
discrete-hedging floor–
spread ÷ floor–

Random numbers, not the paper's data: a short at-the-money call on a $100 index with 20% volatility, worth about $0.50 at the open, hedged at evenly spaced times. The floor is √(π/4)·σ·vega/√N, the standard approximation for the error from hedging N times instead of continuously. Without jumps the spread tracks the floor however often you hedge; with jumps, part of it never hedges away. The paper asks which of the two explains the large errors in 1,243,969 real same-day SPX observations, and finds it is mostly the floor.

clip the tails, count the misses

from R6 · data-dependent winsorization

200 draws from a heavy-tailed distribution, clipped at cutoffs taken from the sample itself. The naive standard error treats those cutoffs as fixed. The re-winsorization bootstrap re-estimates them in every resample.

VII

reading

two or three books a week · short notes on eight

I pick books that will argue with me. If one hasn't disturbed me by page fifty, I read it twice.

I also keep a literary blog for friends. The first five below come up in my personal statement.

B1Virginia Woolf · 1925personal statement

Mrs. Dalloway

Clarissa Dalloway and Septimus Warren Smith never meet. Woolf ties them together anyway: by the clocks striking over London, by the same June day, and finally by the news of Septimus's death reaching Clarissa's party as small talk. His doctors see a case to be managed and miss the person entirely. What stays with me is the method. Woolf moves between minds without warning, so you feel how much of each person never reaches anyone else, even someone on the same bench.

B2Albert Camus · 1942personal statement

The Stranger

Meursault is tried for a killing, but the trial keeps circling back to his mother's funeral, where he didn't cry. The prosecutor builds a monster out of the wrong evidence, and the jury accepts it because the story fits. Meanwhile the man Meursault killed is never even given a name. I read it now as a book about what a verdict leaves out: what the court can't see in Meursault, and what Camus never shows us about his victim.

B3Albert Camus · 1947personal statement

The Plague

Oran shuts its gates and the epidemic turns into a running count of the dead. Dr. Rieux, who turns out to be the narrator, refuses to call his work heroism; it is just his job, done again the next day. My favourite character is Grand, the clerk who spends the whole outbreak rewriting the first sentence of his novel. The book holds both: the numbers that tell you how bad things are, and the single people the numbers can't describe.

B4James Baldwin · 1956personal statement

Giovanni's Room

David tells the whole story in one night, alone in a rented house in the south of France, knowing what is about to happen to Giovanni in Paris. He is unreliable in the most painful way: careful, articulate, and still editing. He keeps describing Giovanni's cramped room as the problem. Baldwin makes clear the problem is David, who would rather lose everyone than be fully known. It is the book that showed me the cost of keeping my own head closed.

B5James Baldwin · 1963personal statement

The Fire Next Time

Two letters. The short one is to Baldwin's nephew on the hundredth anniversary of Emancipation; the long one moves through his years as a teenage preacher and a dinner with Elijah Muhammad. Baldwin argues that America's story about itself depends on what it leaves out, and that the people telling it are trapped by it too. He ends on love, but not a soft kind: love as the decision to see someone completely. I expected anger and got a demand, which is harder to argue with.

B6Robert Greene · 1998

The 48 Laws of Power

Each law comes with a story of someone who broke it and paid, and someone who kept it and won. Read as data, that is a selection problem: the courtiers who followed every law and failed anyway never make it into the book. Greene half admits this in the reversal at the end of each chapter. I don't read it as a manual. I read it as a field guide to how people try to move me, which makes it easier to notice when they do.

B7Samantha Shannon · 2013

The Bone Season

In Scion London in 2059, clairvoyance is a crime, and Paige Mahoney is a dreamwalker working for a gang leader who has ranked every kind of clairvoyant into seven orders. Then she is taken to Sheol I, a hidden penal colony in Oxford, and sorted again, this time by the colour of her tunic. The chart of the seven orders looks neutral. Shannon shows that whoever writes the categories decides who is worth more, which is worth remembering before you label a dataset.

B8Samantha Shannon · 2019–2026

The Priory of the Orange Tree, A Day of Fallen Night, Among the Burning Flowers

The Queendom of Inys believes its royal line keeps a monster chained beneath the sea, so the whole country depends on its queen having an heir. The East worships dragons; the West fears them. Priory spends 800 pages testing the founding story everyone repeats, and the two prequels go back to the moments that story was written: A Day of Fallen Night to the last time the world nearly ended, Among the Burning Flowers to Yscalin as the dragons wake. Three books, one question: what did the official version leave out?
